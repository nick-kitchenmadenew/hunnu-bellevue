## What a Tub-to-Shower Conversion Involves

Removing a bathtub is more involved than it looks from outside the bathroom — a cast iron or heavy acrylic tub is awkward and heavy to get out of a room built around it, and the tub surround that comes with it usually takes wall tile or a fiberglass panel down to the studs. That demolition is also the first real look at the plumbing and framing that's been hidden behind the tub since the house was built, which sometimes surfaces its own issues before the actual conversion work can start.

Once the tub is out, the project becomes building a shower stall largely from scratch — new drain location if the shower footprint doesn't match the tub's, a shower pan or curb set and leveled, and full waterproofing up the walls the way any new shower needs, not a partial patch job around what the tub used to cover. The result is a genuinely new shower, not a tub with the sides removed.

The shower pan itself is worth its own decision — a pre-formed pan set into place, or a tiled shower floor built up on site with its own sloped mortar bed. Pre-formed pans install faster and offer fewer places for water to find a way through; a tiled pan gives more flexibility on size and shape, particularly for a curbless design, but takes more skill to get the slope right toward the drain. Which one fits depends on the shower's shape and the look a homeowner actually wants.

A walk-in or curbless shower — no step to get over, sometimes with a slight floor slope replacing a curb entirely — is one of the more common reasons homeowners request this conversion, particularly for a primary bathroom where stepping over a tub wall daily has become a real inconvenience. That style has its own plumbing and waterproofing requirements beyond a standard curbed shower pan, worth discussing specifically if it's the goal rather than assumed to be included in any tub removal.

Accessibility is a related but distinct reason people request this conversion — a curbless entry and a shower bench are both easier to use for anyone with limited mobility than a tub someone has to step over, and that's worth raising directly at the design consultation if it's part of why the conversion is being considered, since it can shape decisions like curb height and grab-bar blocking that are much easier to plan for now than to retrofit later once the walls are already finished.

## Rerouting Plumbing for the New Shower

A tub's drain and a shower's drain rarely sit in exactly the same place, since a tub drain is positioned at one end of a roughly 60-inch fixture and a shower drain is typically centered in a smaller footprint. That difference means the drain line under the floor usually has to move, which is plumbing work that happens before any waterproofing or tile goes in, and it's part of why a conversion takes real planning rather than being a simple fixture swap.

Supply valve height and position change too — a tub filler sits low, near where someone would be seated, while a shower valve sits at standing height, typically centered in the new stall rather than at the tub's original faucet location. Both the drain and supply work get roughed in and, where required, inspected before the visible shower stall — pan, walls, tile — goes in on top of it.

For a conversion that's also adding features like a built-in bench, a niche, or multiple shower heads, the rough-in plumbing has to account for those from the start — a bench needs blocking and waterproofing built around its shape, and a second shower head or body sprays need their own supply lines run before the walls close up. None of that is something to decide once tile work has already started.

## Waterproofing a New Shower Stall

Because a tub-to-shower conversion builds an entirely new wet area, waterproofing gets done from scratch rather than assessed and partially reused the way it might be in a straightforward shower retile. Every wall the new shower spray reaches gets a proper membrane before tile or a shower panel goes up, extended past the shower opening itself per most code requirements, with particular attention to the shower pan's connection to the walls around it — one of the more common failure points in any shower, new or converted.

A shower curb, if the design uses one instead of a curbless entry, needs its own waterproofing detail where it meets both the pan and the walls on either side — a corner that's easy to shortcut and hard to notice has been shortcut until water starts finding its way past it. This is the same discipline any bathroom tile job needs, just applied to a stall that didn't exist in this footprint the week before.

## Tub-to-Shower Conversion as a Standalone Project vs Part of a Renovation

Most tub-to-shower conversions run as their own focused project — the vanity, flooring and rest of the bathroom stay exactly as they are while the conversion itself gets scoped, quoted and completed on its own timeline. That's the more common request, and it's a faster, more contained project than touching the whole room.

It becomes part of a larger renovation when the vanity or flooring are changing at the same time, since planning the new shower's waterproofing and drain location alongside those other trades avoids working around fixtures that would otherwise need protecting during a separate later project. Either way, the same crew handles the demolition, the plumbing rerouting and the waterproofing and tile that finish the new shower — one team accountable for the whole conversion rather than a plumber and a tile setter each responsible for only their own piece of it.

A homeowner unsure whether their bathroom is a good candidate for this kind of conversion doesn't need to know in advance — the design consultation is where the existing plumbing, framing and layout get assessed honestly against what a walk-in shower would actually require in that specific room, before any quote is written or any assumptions are made about what the room can actually support once the walls are opened up.
