## Kitchen Remodeling in Issaquah

Issaquah sits east of Bellevue at the base of the Cascade foothills, and it's a city with a genuine mix of housing ages — newer master-planned neighborhoods alongside streets that have been there for decades closer to the historic downtown. Hunnu Construction has served the whole range since 2018, and the starting point is the same regardless: an in-home design consultation to see what the kitchen actually is, not an assumption based on the neighborhood.

That mix matters because the two ends of it usually need different things. A newer Issaquah kitchen often shipped with builder-grade cabinets and counters chosen to a construction budget rather than a homeowner's taste, and the most common request there is an upgrade — better cabinets, a different countertop material — without the layout itself changing. An older Issaquah kitchen more often needs the fuller conversation: whether the layout still works for how the house is used now, on top of finishes that have simply worn out.

## Planning an Issaquah Kitchen Project

Whichever situation applies, the project starts the same way: a design consultation to measure the space and talk through what's actually needed, followed by an honest scope — a single trade if that's genuinely all the kitchen needs, or a full renovation if more than one part of it has to change. Neither gets sold as the answer when the other one actually fits.

From there, the sequence is consistent across every Issaquah project: cabinets set and confirmed first, countertops templated to them once they're in place, flooring and backsplash finishing the room in the order that protects the surfaces already installed. One crew carries that sequence the whole way, whether the job is a two-day cabinet upgrade or a multi-week full renovation.

## Builder-Grade Isn't a Permanent Choice

A kitchen that came with the house is often the last room a new owner thinks to change, since nothing about it is technically broken — the cabinets close, the counter is intact, the floor holds up. But builder-grade finishes are chosen to a construction budget, not to any particular homeowner's taste, and "nothing's broken" isn't the same as "this is what we'd choose."

The upgrade path from there is usually simpler than it sounds. If the layout already works, swapping the cabinets, the countertop, or both doesn't require touching plumbing, electrical or the floor plan — it's a matter of choosing materials that actually fit the house rather than the ones a builder chose off a standard list, and scheduling the swap around what's already a functioning kitchen rather than a broken one.

## Kitchens That Open to the Outdoors

A number of newer Issaquah homes are built with the kitchen opening onto a deck or patio, which changes a few practical things about a remodel that a fully interior kitchen doesn't have to consider — sliding or French doors near the work area, sightlines from the kitchen through to outdoor space, and sometimes an island or peninsula oriented toward that view rather than toward the rest of the interior.

None of that changes the trades involved, but it does change what the design consultation actually discusses. A cabinet run or countertop overhang near a doorway to the outside gets measured with that traffic pattern in mind, and a kitchen that functions as the hub between indoor and outdoor space is designed around that role rather than around a generic layout that happens to have a door in it.

Backsplash and countertop material choices sometimes get weighed a little differently in this kind of layout too, since a kitchen with a door standing open through much of the year sees more temperature swing and moisture near that opening than a fully enclosed one would. It's a minor factor next to the bigger layout questions, but worth mentioning honestly at the design consultation rather than assumed away without discussion, especially on a kitchen that opens directly onto a deck or patio used most of the year in typical Pacific Northwest weather. Raising it early costs nothing and avoids a surprise later, which is the same principle behind most of what gets covered at that first visit.
