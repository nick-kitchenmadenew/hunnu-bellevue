## What Backsplash Installation Involves

Backsplash is the last trade in a kitchen for a simple reason: it has to meet both the countertop below it and the cabinets on either side, so both need to already be in their finished position before tile can be measured and set against them. Install it any earlier and it's guessing at where those surfaces will actually land — a backsplash set before the countertop is confirmed is a backsplash that may not close the gap it's there to close.

It's a smaller-scale version of the same rule that governs every trade in a kitchen remodel: measure against what's actually there, not against a plan drawn up before anyone confirmed it. A countertop and cabinet run that's off by even a small amount changes exactly where the backsplash has to start and stop, which is why it waits until both are finished rather than being scheduled to run in parallel with them.

That gap is also the hardest-working strip of surface in the kitchen. It sits directly behind the range, the sink and the counter where prep happens, taking more daily splash, grease and heat than almost anywhere else in the room, which is exactly why it's tiled rather than left as painted drywall — a painted wall behind a stove doesn't hold up the way tile does, and it shows the wear within a year or two.

Wall prep matters as much as the tile itself. Whatever surface the tile is set against needs to be flat, clean and sound — old paint, old adhesive or an uneven wall left over from a previous backsplash all have to be addressed first, since tile set over a compromised surface can fail even when the tile work itself is done well.

## Choosing Tile, Layout and Grout

Tile choice is where a backsplash project genuinely differs from the trades ahead of it: it's the one surface in the kitchen where pattern, not just material, is a real design decision. A straight running-bond layout, a herringbone pattern, a vertical stack — the same tile looks like a different kitchen depending on how it's laid, and that choice gets made during the design consultation before any tile is set, not improvised on installation day.

Grout color matters more than it seems like it should. A grout close to the tile's own color reads as one continuous surface; a contrasting grout makes the pattern and the individual tiles the visible feature instead. Neither is more correct than the other — it's a real choice with a real effect on the finished look, and one worth deciding deliberately rather than defaulting to whatever's easiest to keep clean.

Grout type is a separate decision from color, and it affects maintenance more than looks. Some grouts resist staining and moisture better than others, which matters more behind a range or sink than it does on a bathroom floor — the backsplash is exactly the surface in the house most exposed to the kind of daily splash and grease that a poorly chosen grout struggles against over time.

## Where the Backsplash Stops

Height and coverage are decided before tile goes up, not discovered as the installer works up the wall. A standard-height backsplash — a modest band above the counter — is the most common choice, but a full-height backsplash running from counter to the underside of the upper cabinets is a different, bolder look, and it uses considerably more tile. Both are legitimate choices; the design consultation is where the tradeoff in cost and look gets weighed honestly.

Around windows, outlets and the range hood, the backsplash has to be cut to fit cleanly rather than simply run up to the obstacle and stop — an outlet cover set flush against the tile, a window trim that meets the tile edge cleanly, are the kind of details that separate a finished-looking backsplash from one that reads as tile applied around whatever was already on the wall.

A range hood, if the kitchen has one, is usually the single most complicated cut in the whole installation — tile has to work around its shape and mounting hardware while still reading as a deliberate part of the pattern rather than an interruption in it. It's worth confirming the hood's exact position before tile work starts, since moving it after the backsplash is set means redoing the cuts around it.

## Backsplash Installation as a Standalone Project vs Part of a Renovation

A standalone backsplash installation is the right call when the countertop and cabinets are staying and only the wall between them needs updating — a common project for a kitchen where the counter was replaced years ago but the backsplash was never redone to match, or where the original backsplash has simply worn out or gone out of style. It's the fastest of the five ways to work with Hunnu Construction, and it doesn't require touching anything else in the room.

Inside a full renovation, backsplash is still the last trade, following the same logic — it goes in once the new countertop is set and confirmed, closing the gap between counter and cabinets with tile chosen to work with both. Whether it's the final step of a full renovation or a standalone update on its own, the same design consultation covers tile, layout, grout and height before anything goes on the wall.

A homeowner replacing only the backsplash doesn't need to know in advance whether the existing countertop and cabinets will actually work with the tile they have in mind — that's a normal question to bring to the design consultation, not something to resolve before calling.

Bringing photos or samples of an inspiration tile helps that conversation, but the final decision still gets made against the actual kitchen — the specific counter color, the cabinet finish, the light the room gets during the day — since a tile that reads one way in a photo can read differently once it's actually held up against the wall it's going on.

Timing a backsplash update is also worth raising early if it's not urgent. Because it's the fastest of the five services to schedule, it's often the easiest one to fit around a homeowner's own calendar rather than the crew's — a detail worth mentioning during the first call rather than assuming the project has to start as soon as possible.

It's also a common project to pair with a smaller cosmetic refresh — new hardware, a fresh coat of paint on the walls — timed around the same short window the backsplash work already takes, rather than scheduled as three separate disruptions to the kitchen spread out over months.
