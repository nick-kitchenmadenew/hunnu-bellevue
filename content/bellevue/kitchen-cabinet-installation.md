## What Cabinet Installation Involves

Cabinet installation starts with measuring the room against the actual layout being installed — not the layout on paper, but the walls, corners and floor as they actually are, which are rarely perfectly square in a home that's been standing for any length of time. That gap between the drawing and the room is exactly what shims, scribe strips and careful leveling exist to close, and it's the part of the job that separates cabinets that look built-in from cabinets that look like boxes set against a wall.

Base cabinets go in before uppers in most layouts, since they give the crew a stable, level reference to measure up from — uppers hung first, with nothing below to check against, are how a kitchen ends up with a visible gap between the top of the base cabinets and the bottom of the uppers on one side of the room and not the other. Corner cabinets, if the layout has one, usually go in first of all, since every other run in the kitchen gets measured off of it.

Whether the cabinets are stock, semi-custom or fully custom changes how much of this is decided ahead of time versus adjusted on site. Stock cabinets come in fixed sizes, so the layout has to be built around what's available; custom and semi-custom boxes can be sized to the room, which shifts more of the decision-making into the design consultation up front and less into on-site adjustment once installation starts.

## Setting Cabinets Level and Square

Level and square sound like the same thing and aren't. Level means each cabinet sits flat rather than tilting forward or back; square means the whole run sits at a true right angle to the wall and to the cabinets next to it. A cabinet can be perfectly level and still be out of square with its neighbor, and that's the error that shows up later as a countertop that doesn't sit flush or a backsplash tile course that visibly tapers across the wall.

Every cabinet gets secured into wall studs, not just into drywall — drywall alone won't hold the weight of a loaded upper cabinet over years of use, and finding the studs behind existing drywall is part of the walkthrough before installation starts. Shims go behind and under the cabinet to correct for a wall or floor that isn't perfectly flat, which almost none are, and getting that shimming right the first time is what everything installed afterward — counters, backsplash, even how well the doors hang — ends up depending on.

Cabinets get screwed together at shared sides before they're secured to the wall, so a whole run acts as one rigid unit rather than several boxes each holding their own weight independently. That's part of why a cabinet installation is done in a specific order rather than wherever is most convenient in the room — a box installed out of sequence can throw off the alignment of every box installed after it, which is far harder to correct once the run is finished than to get right the first time.

## Doors, Drawers and Hardware Last

Cabinet boxes go in early, but doors, drawers and hardware go on last, after the room's other trades — countertop, flooring, backsplash — are finished. That order isn't arbitrary: doors and drawer fronts are the easiest thing in the kitchen to scuff, chip or scratch, and there's no reason to have them hanging while other trades are still moving material and tools through the room.

Once everything else is in, doors get hung and adjusted — reveals (the gap between adjacent doors) and alignment get checked and fine-tuned, since factory hinges rarely sit perfectly out of the box. Hardware — handles and pulls — goes on last of all, positioned and drilled to the layout chosen during the design consultation, which is also when spacing and placement on drawers of different sizes gets decided so the whole run reads as consistent rather than measured door by door.

This is also the point where soft-close hinges and drawer glides get checked if the cabinets came with them — a hinge that slams or a drawer that doesn't glide smoothly is easiest to catch and adjust before the kitchen is back in daily use, rather than after, when the same fix means emptying a drawer that's now full.

## Cabinet Installation as Its Own Project vs Part of a Renovation

A standalone cabinet installation makes sense when the layout itself is staying the same — same footprint, same sink location, same run of counter — and only the cabinets have reached the end of their life or no longer suit the kitchen. The existing countertop can sometimes be reused if the new cabinet depth and height match closely enough, though in most cases a countertop that was templated to the old cabinets won't fit the new ones without at least some rework, which is worth discussing honestly during the design consultation rather than assuming either way.

The same goes for the existing floor and backsplash. A floor that already runs to the base of the old cabinets should reach the new ones too, as long as the new boxes sit at the same depth — a deeper or shallower cabinet run changes that math. A backsplash tied to the old countertop height is the one most likely to need at least partial rework, since it has to close the same gap against a countertop that hasn't moved.

Where a standalone installation stops making sense is when the layout itself needs to change — moving the sink, adding an island, reconfiguring where the range sits. At that point the plumbing and electrical behind the walls need to move too, which is rough-in work that goes beyond a cabinet-only project and turns it into a full renovation. Neither is the wrong starting point — the design consultation is where it gets sorted out which one actually fits the kitchen in front of it.

A homeowner who isn't sure which category their project falls into doesn't need to decide that before calling. Describing what's actually wrong with the current kitchen — the layout, the cabinets, or both — is enough to start from, and the walkthrough is where it gets narrowed down to an actual scope of work.

That same walkthrough is also where cabinet style and finish get discussed — shaker, flat-panel, painted, stained — none of which changes the installation sequence, but all of which are easier to choose well once the crew has actually seen the room's light and layout rather than a photo taken to send along ahead of the visit.
