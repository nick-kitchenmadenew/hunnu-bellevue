## What a Full Kitchen Renovation Covers

A full kitchen renovation is the answer when the kitchen needs more than one thing fixed — the layout doesn't work, the cabinets are worn out, the counters are cracked, and the floor and backsplash have never matched the rest. Rather than tackle each of those as a separate project with a separate contractor, a renovation treats the room as one job: one design consultation to measure the space and choose cabinets, countertops, flooring and backsplash together, and one crew that carries that plan through demolition, rough-in, installation and the final walkthrough.

That's the real difference between a renovation and a single-trade project. Replacing a countertop on its own means working around cabinets, a floor and a layout that all stay exactly as they are. A renovation means none of those are fixed points — the layout itself can move, a wall can come out, and every surface in the room gets chosen to work with every other surface, rather than matched to whatever was already there. It's a bigger project, and it's the right one when more than one part of the kitchen has reached the end of its life at the same time, which is common in a kitchen that hasn't been touched since the house was built.

It's also the project type where a design consultation earns the most, because more decisions are actually open. On a single-trade job, the countertop has to fit the cabinets that already exist — the design conversation is mostly about material and color. On a renovation, the cabinet layout itself is still a decision, which means the counter, the sink placement, and how much space each part of the kitchen gets are all being decided together rather than each one working around a choice already made by the last project.

## Demolition and What's Behind the Walls

A renovation is also the project type most likely to touch what's behind the walls, which is why it starts with demolition rather than straight to installation. Old cabinets, counters and flooring come out first, which is also when the crew can actually see the subfloor condition, the state of the plumbing under the sink, and whether the electrical behind the range and counter outlets is up to what the new layout needs.

This is the stage where a renovation genuinely differs from a single-trade job: moving a sink or a range means moving the plumbing or the gas line that feeds it, and a new layout with more counter outlets means running new circuits before any new surface goes back in. None of that is visible from the finished kitchen, but it's the work that decides whether the new layout actually functions the way it was designed to — a beautiful new island with no outlet in it because the rough-in wasn't rerun is a mistake made at the design stage, not the installation stage, which is exactly why it gets addressed at the design consultation before demolition ever starts.

Demolition is also when a subfloor problem gets found, if there is one — water damage under an old dishwasher, or a floor that isn't level, neither of which is visible until the existing flooring and cabinets are out of the way. Finding it here, before new flooring goes down, is the difference between fixing it once and fixing it twice.

## How the Trades Are Sequenced in a Renovation

Once rough-in is done and inspected where it needs to be, the visible trades go in, in an order that protects each one from the trade after it. Cabinets go first — every other surface in the kitchen gets measured and cut to fit around them, so they have to be set level, square, and in their final position before anything else starts. Countertops are templated only once the cabinets underneath are confirmed in place, because a template taken off boxes that shift even slightly afterward produces a countertop that doesn't sit flush.

Flooring and backsplash finish the room last, and the order between them depends on the specific layout — flooring generally runs first so it reaches cleanly to the base of the cabinetry rather than being cut around cabinets set on top of it, and backsplash goes in once the countertop below it is set, since it has to run tight against that edge. A renovation that skips this sequence — installing flooring before cabinets, or tiling backsplash before the counter is confirmed — is how a job ends up with visible gaps and seams that a homeowner notices for years after the crew is gone.

Keeping one crew across all four trades is what makes this sequence hold. When a homeowner books a cabinet installer, then separately books a countertop company once the cabinets are in, then separately books a flooring crew, each new contractor is scheduling around their own calendar rather than around the renovation's — and any one of them running late pushes back everyone booked after them.

## When a Full Renovation Makes Sense vs a Single Trade

Not every outdated kitchen needs a full renovation. If the layout works, the cabinets are structurally sound, and only one surface has actually failed — a cracked countertop, flooring that's worn through, a backsplash that was never finished — replacing that one thing on its own is a smaller, faster and less expensive project, and Hunnu Construction takes those on just as often as full renovations.

A renovation is the right call when more than one of those is true at once, or when the layout itself is the problem — not enough counter space, an island that doesn't fit the room, a sink under a window that was never actually used. At that point, fixing one surface without addressing the others usually just means redoing that same surface again in a few years once the rest of the kitchen catches up to it. The design consultation is where that gets sorted out honestly — a renovation isn't sold as the answer to a problem a single trade would actually solve.

There's no wrong answer to start from. A homeowner unsure which category their project falls into doesn't need to decide before calling — that's exactly what the first walkthrough is for, and it costs nothing to find out that a full renovation isn't actually needed.
